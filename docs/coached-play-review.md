# Coached play review

Age is a starting point, not a measurement of ability. The configurations below are product defaults chosen to make the task smaller or more demanding. They are not validated developmental norms or a claim that a child of a particular age should master a skill.

This review combines software playthroughs with an adult assessment of likely reading, memory, motor and reasoning demands. No children participated. Enjoyment, independent comprehension and sustained interest need observed child playtesting.

## What the coach does

Every activity has a starting instruction, a strategy, a conversation prompt and a related activity with real objects. The in-game hint demonstrates or exposes the current step where that is meaningful. Young modes keep models visible and invite a grown-up to read and demonstrate. There are no countdowns or penalties for retries.

Each activity remembers its own adjustment, up to two practice steps below or above the selected starting point, within ages 2–10. The actual age setting does not change. Changing a challenge starts a new round; drawing edits remain recoverable. Read-aloud is optional and works through the native iOS voice bridge in the app. Practice stars count activity completion; they do not measure independent mastery.

## Nine starting points

| Starting age | Quantity ceiling | Trace path tolerance / coverage | Typical literacy task | Intended use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 3 | 8.5% / 60% | Modelled two-letter matching | Shared exploration: marks, matching, one action at a time |
| 3 | 4 | 7.5% / 66% | Modelled three-letter words | Name pictures and count small groups together |
| 4 | 5 | 6.5% / 72% | Three letters with an extra choice | Short sequences and simple rules with visible support |
| 5 | 8 | 5.5% / 76% | Familiar three-letter words | Connect symbols to pictures and quantities |
| 6 | 10 | 4.5% / 80% | Four-letter words and four case pairs | Try a strategy and explain a choice |
| 7 | 12 | 4.0% / 82% | Four-letter words with a distractor | Longer tasks and less automatic modelling |
| 8 | 15 | 3.6% / 85% | Five-letter words and five case pairs | Multiple steps, skip sequences and spatial planning |
| 9 | 20 | 3.2% / 88% | Six-letter words and six case pairs | More complex patterns and number relationships |
| 10 | 20 | 2.8% / 90% | Eight-letter compound words | Inverse questions, descending skip sequences and explanations |

Trace tolerance is relative to the drawing board; coverage is measured along the path. Precision and excess-ink checks also apply. These are input forgiveness settings, not handwriting assessments. The oldest quantity ceiling stays at 20 to protect phone readability; challenge increases through reasoning rather than more tiny objects.

## Activity-by-activity assessment

The final verification section records what ran. The expectations in this table are design judgments, not observations of children.

| Activity | Likely younger-child experience (2–4) | Middle years (5–7) | Older-child challenge (8–10) / remaining limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Doodle studio | Immediate marks, large brushes and simple prompts | Combine shapes, seasons, detail and repeating borders | Perspective, inventions and visual storytelling; quality remains self-directed |
| Color & create | Choose a picture and tap Fill; shared color naming | Deliberate colors and pen details | Use color and composition intentionally; filling alone is a relaxing warm-up |
| Line & shape trails | Generous paths and a replayable model | Smoother connected curves | More precise control; simple paths remain a warm-up |
| Big letter trails | Shared letter/mark exploration, not a reading prerequisite | Notice stroke families and letter formation | Precision practice; fluent writers may prefer story and word activities |
| Little letter trails | Adult models curves and lifts | Distinguish similar letter forms | Accurate formation and fine control; tracing does not assess independent writing |
| Word trails | Copy familiar letter shapes together | Trace a whole familiar word | Longer sustained motor control; vocabulary is intentionally limited |
| Number trails | Relate a numeral to fingers or objects together | Formation with less forgiveness | Precision warm-up, not an advanced numeracy challenge |
| Count with me | Tap and name up to a few objects | One-to-one counting and keeping track | Larger structured groups; counting alone may be easy for fluent older children |
| Add together | Combine small visible groups with a grown-up | Count on and connect objects to equations | Larger totals and three-addend tasks, while retaining object support |
| Equal groups | Share/count a few objects together | Repeated equal quantities | Reason about group size and total; concrete support stays available |
| Shape detective | Match visible shapes with few choices | Recognize names and defining features | Property reasoning and vocabulary; familiar shapes remain useful warm-ups |
| Color buddies | Match a swatch and name a color together | Distinguish a broader palette | Color relationships where offered; recognition remains a low-pressure activity |
| Pattern parade | Say and extend short repeating patterns | Find AB, AAB and ABC units | Longer, less obvious repeat units; ask for the rule rather than rapid guessing |
| Sort it out | Sort a few familiar pictures into two groups | Classify more objects and categories | Distinguish transport categories; explain the rule and alternative rules |
| Spot the difference | Attend to one explicit feature | Compare shape or color | Compare quantities/features among distractors; speed is not scored |
| Memory garden | A small set of pairs; wrong pairs stay up until ready | Keep track of more positions | More pairs and deliberate memory strategies; repetition may become familiar |
| Little pathfinder | Few large cells, legal moves highlighted | Plan a route and undo dead ends | Longer paths and planning; random layouts add replay value |
| More, less, same | Pair small groups with a grown-up | Compare quantities and equality | Explain the difference between groups and check relationships |
| Number stepping stones | A short ascending path | Longer sequences and gaps | Skip sequences and descending order; reading the direction matters |
| Take away | Remove one visible item and recount | Count remaining objects without crossed-out ones | Work backwards to find what was removed as well as what remains |
| Missing number | Reveal the missing part visually | Count on to a whole | Number bonds with larger wholes; verbal strategy adds challenge |
| Fill the frame | Make small quantities in a five-frame | Use rows of five and a ten-frame | Reason from empty spaces and a twenty-frame; fixed-size targets stay tappable |
| Letter buddies | One or a few visible case models | Pair more forms by name | Similar letter shapes and more pairs; letter recognition remains a warm-up for fluent readers |
| Build a word | Match two/three letters to a visible word model together | Familiar words, repeated letters and extra choices | Longer/compound words and distractors; a model remains available |
| Growing garden | Compare a small number of visibly different sizes | Order several sizes | More comparisons and closer sizes; size order remains concrete |
| Story steps | Talk through a short familiar routine | Explain dependencies among several events | More steps and causal explanations; routines should be discussed if unfamiliar |
| Follow the arrows | Follow one visible direction at a time | Keep a place in a longer route | Multi-step spatial planning; no reading-only route requirement |
| Shape builder | Join a few visible corners with a model | Count sides and close an outline | More vertices and spatial distractors; avoids tiny drawing targets |
| Tap the pattern | Copy a short visual sequence without a timer | Hold and reproduce a longer sequence | Longer sequence memory; this tests order, not musical timing |
| Fair shares | Give one item to each friend in turn | Distribute larger groups equally | Work out equal shares and leftovers; retain a concrete check |

## Basis for the approach

NAEYC recommends adapting support to individual development and describes hints, modelling and changing materials as forms of scaffolding. That supports giving a child a visible model and adjustable support rather than treating age as a pass/fail gate. [NAEYC: Teaching to Enhance Each Child’s Development and Learning](https://www.naeyc.org/node/3812).

The American Academy of Pediatrics describes shared media use as an opportunity for adults to discuss what children see, build and learn. The coach’s conversation and real-object prompts follow that principle. [AAP: Watch Together](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/watch-together.aspx).
